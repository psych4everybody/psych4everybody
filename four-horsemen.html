The Four Horsemen, Up Close

Why some arguments do lasting damage, what to say instead, and how much of the famous research holds up.

About 14 minutes to readOne 20-minute practiceBuilds on Section C

In John Gottman's lab, which told researchers more about a couple's future: how often they argued, or how they argued?

How they argued. Some of the most stable couples Gottman studied argued often and loudly; others hardly argued at all. What marked the couples who were heading for trouble was the manner of their arguments: criticism, contempt, defensiveness and shutting down, and too little warmth to balance them.

Key points

  1. The four horsemen are about how couples fight, not what they fight about. Criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling can turn up in an argument about anything, from money to the dishwasher.
  2. They tend to arrive together. An attack invites a defense, the defense invites a sharper attack, and someone eventually shuts down.
  3. Each has a practical alternative. The alternatives are easiest to use early, before anyone is too wound up to think.
  4. The patterns are well supported; the famous percentages are not. Claims that a researcher can predict divorce with over 90% accuracy are far shakier than the patterns behind them.
  5. Better communication is not the whole story. Happier couples communicate better, and better communication helps couples stay happy, but neither link is as strong as popular advice suggests.

By the end of this page you will be able to

  • Recognize each horseman in a real conversation, including your own.
  • Say what you could do instead, and why it helps.
  • Take a break during an argument without it turning into stonewalling.
  • Explain what Gottman's research shows, and what it cannot show.
Part 1

What each horseman sounds like

And what you could say instead.

Section C introduced Gottman's four horsemen. Here we slow down and look at each one in an ordinary argument, with an alternative you could try and the reason it tends to work. Gottman's team calls these alternatives antidotes.

Strong for the patterns: the four behaviors are linked to unhappiness and breakup, and not only in Gottman's lab. A study following married couples for 16 years found that those who routinely fought this way were more likely to divorce (Birditt and colleagues, 2010).
Mixed for the alternatives: each one fits the wider research, but few have been tested on their own in controlled trials.

1. Criticism

What it is: turning a complaint about something your partner did into a verdict on who they are. Words like "always", "never" and "you're so..." are the giveaway.

Instead of"You always leave everything to me. You're so selfish."
Try a gentle start-up"I'm swamped tonight. Could you do bath and bedtime while I finish the kitchen?"

Why this works: it names one situation, says how you feel, and asks for something specific. A behavior can be changed; a character can only be defended. How a conversation starts also matters: when Sybil Carrère and Gottman coded newlyweds' arguments, the first three minutes alone predicted how the couples were doing six years later.

2. Contempt

What it is: speaking from a position of superiority: mockery, sarcasm, eye-rolling, sneering, name-calling. Criticism says "you did something wrong"; contempt says "you are beneath me". Gottman considers it the most damaging of the four.

Instead of(eye-roll) "Oh, here we go. The world expert on loading a dishwasher has spoken."
Try describing your own feelings and needs"I feel judged when you redo the dishwasher after I've loaded it. Can we agree either way is fine?"

Why this works: it turns a jab back into a complaint you can actually discuss. The longer-term antidote is to notice and say what you appreciate about your partner, often. In close relationships, bad moments weigh more than good ones (Roy Baumeister and colleagues called this "bad is stronger than good"), so it takes a lot of warmth to offset a little scorn. Gottman's team suggests contempt grows out of negative thoughts about a partner that have simmered for a long time, so raising complaints early, while they are still small, may also help.

3. Defensiveness

What it is: protecting yourself from a complaint by making excuses, playing the innocent victim, or counter-attacking. It is a natural reaction to being criticized, which is why criticism and defensiveness so often come as a pair.

Instead of"I was going to pay it! And you never remind me about anything anyway."
Try taking responsibility, even for part of it"You're right, I forgot the electricity bill. I'll pay it now and set a reminder."

Why this works: defensiveness turns one complaint into two, and the conversation becomes the cross-complaining you met in Section C. Accepting even a small share of the problem gives your partner nothing to push against. In research on what helps couples negotiate, accepting responsibility and offering concessions are among the clearest helpful tactics (Canary and Lakey, 2013).

Explaining is not always defendingGiving context ("I forgot because the reminder email went to spam") is not automatically defensive. It becomes defensive when it is used to deny any responsibility, or when it comes with a counter-attack.

4. Stonewalling

What it is: withdrawing from the conversation: going silent, looking away, turning to your phone, or walking out without a word. People who stonewall often feel they are calming things down. To the other person it usually looks like icy indifference.

Instead ofStaring at your phone in silence, then leaving the room.
Try a break with a return time"I'm too wound up to listen properly right now. I do want to sort this out. Can we come back to it at eight?"

Why this works: stonewalling is often a sign that someone is flooded, so overwhelmed that they cannot think straight. A break is the right instinct. Saying when you will come back is what makes it a break rather than a rejection. Without a return time, the other partner tends to push harder, and the pair slide into the demand-withdraw pattern from Section C.

How they chain together

Rowland Miller's textbook describes a typical sequence. A clumsy criticism attacks the partner's character. The partner defends themselves, often with a counter-complaint. Contempt creeps in as each tries to land a harder blow. Eventually one person shuts down. Miller also adds a fifth behavior, belligerence: openly provocative challenges such as "And what exactly are you going to do about it?"

This is why the start of an argument matters so much. Each horseman makes the next one more likely, so the easiest place to change direction is the first sentence.

Everyone does this sometimesGottman's own team stresses that a bit of criticism does not mean a relationship is doomed. What matters is how often the horsemen appear, whether contempt becomes a habit, and whether couples can repair things afterwards.
Part 2

Try it

One practice for the moment an argument overheats.
Practice

The 20-minute reset Mixed

At least 20 minutesWhen an argument overheatsModerate: needs agreeing in advance

A planned break that stops flooding and stonewalling from taking over, without leaving the problem unsolved.

How to do it

  1. Agree on it while you are both calm. Choose a signal phrase ("I need a reset") and agree that either of you can use it, and that the other will respect it.
  2. Notice the signs. A racing heart, a hot face, repeating yourself louder, or no longer following what your partner is saying.
  3. Name it and set a return time. "I need a reset. Let's come back to this at eight."
  4. Calm your body, apart from each other. Slow your breathing to about six breaths a minute, take a walk, or read something unrelated. Give it at least 20 minutes.
  5. Don't rehearse the argument. Replaying "how dare they" keeps you flooded. If you do think about it, picture how a fair-minded friend who wants the best for both of you would see it.
  6. Come back on time, and start gently. Opening with what you heard helps: "I think what upset you was..."

Why it works

A flooded person loses their sense of humor, their ability to take in new information, and much of their capacity to problem-solve. More talking does not help until the body settles. Gottman describes stopping couples mid-argument in his lab and having them read magazines for 20 minutes, until their heart rates returned to normal. When they resumed, they were noticeably calmer, more reasonable, and kinder.

How good is the evidence?

Mixed. The magazine demonstration comes from Gottman's own book rather than a published controlled trial. But Miller's textbook reports other studies finding that short breaks help conflicts go more smoothly when people come back to them, and that thinking about a disagreement from a neutral third person's point of view is linked to happier relationships a year later.

Keep in mind. A break only works if you come back. Leaving with no return time can feel like abandonment, especially to a partner who already worries about being left. And this is a tool for heated arguments between two people who feel safe with each other. If you are ever afraid of how your partner will react, it is not the right tool; see the box below.
Part 3

What the research actually shows

Where the horsemen came from, and how far the findings stretch.
Inside the study

Gottman and Levenson (1992): watching couples argue

What they did

In 1983, John Gottman and Robert Levenson recruited couples through newspaper advertisements in Bloomington, Indiana. Each couple discussed an ongoing disagreement for 15 minutes. Researchers coded every speaking turn as positive or negative and recorded the partners' heart rates and other body signals. Four years later, they checked on the 73 couples they could follow up.

What they found

Couples whose conversations stayed more positive than negative did better. The others were more defensive, more stubborn and withdrawn, less satisfied, in poorer health four years later, and more likely to have considered separating or to have actually separated.

What it can't tell us
  • How far it travels. The couples came from one US town in the early 1980s. By the 1987 follow-up only about 9% had divorced, so any percentage about divorce rests on a handful of couples.
  • Cause and effect. The study shows that negativity and later trouble go together, not that one causes the other. Unhappy couples may argue badly because they are unhappy.
  • Accuracy for your relationship. A pattern that is common in struggling couples cannot tell you, on its own, whether a particular couple will split up.

Where the "over 90% accurate" claims went wrong Mixed

You may have read that Gottman can predict divorce with over 90% accuracy from a short conversation. The patterns behind that claim are real. The percentages are not as solid as they sound.

Richard Heyman and Amy Smith Slep showed why. They built a divorce "predictor" using nothing about how couples talk, only basic facts such as education, employment, drinking and young children. On the couples they used to build it, it was right about 90% of the time. Then they tested it on a fresh group. When it predicted a couple would divorce, it was right only about 29% of the time.

The problem is called overfitting. A formula tuned to one group of people learns that group's quirks as well as any real pattern, so it always looks better on the people it was built from. The fix is to test it on new people, and the famous divorce-prediction studies had not done that. Heyman and Slep also pointed out that one of those studies based its predictions on just 60 couples.

Does good communication cause a happy relationship? Mixed

Most relationship advice assumes that better communication leads to a happier relationship. Justin Lavner, Benjamin Karney and Thomas Bradbury tested this with more than 400 low-income newlywed couples in Los Angeles, observing them four times over about three years.

At any one time, happier couples communicated better. But over time, the links in both directions were weak. Communication did not reliably predict later happiness, and when one link was stronger, it was more often happiness predicting communication than the reverse.

This does not mean the horsemen don't matter. It means communication is one part of a bigger picture that also includes stress, money, health, and what else is going on in a couple's life. Learning better scripts is worth doing; it is not a cure-all.

Therapy-speak decoder

Gottman's terms have spread far beyond the research. Online, they often mean something different.

"You're stonewalling me."
Online, it often meansAny silence: not replying to a text, wanting space, or ending a conversation.
In the research, it meansShutting down in the middle of a conflict conversation, usually because you are overwhelmed. Saying "I need a break; let's talk at eight" is the antidote, not stonewalling.
"That's criticism."
Online, it often meansAny complaint, or any negative feedback at all.
In the research, it meansAn attack on someone's character. Complaints about specific behavior are healthy, and avoiding them can make things worse; Section D explains why speaking up beats quietly waiting.
"One of the horsemen showed up, so we're doomed."
Online, it often meansA single bad argument is a red flag for the whole relationship.
In the research, it meansMost couples show these behaviors sometimes. The warning signs are a habit of contempt, and couples who cannot repair things afterwards.

When this isn't the right tool

Everything on this page assumes two people who both want the conversation to go well and who feel safe with each other. Sometimes that is not true. Silence, contempt and constant criticism can also be used deliberately, to punish, frighten or control a partner. That is a pattern of control, not a communication problem, and better scripts will not fix it.

If you feel afraid of how your partner will react, if you are being cut off from people, monitored, or put down as a pattern, read "When it is not a communication problem" in Section D. A domestic abuse service in your country can help you think through options safely. If you are in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number.

Think about it

  • Think of an argument you would rather forget. What was the very first sentence? Rewrite it as a gentle start-up.
  • When you feel criticized, what is your first instinct: to explain, to counter-attack, or to go quiet?
  • Is there one thing on this page you will actually do differently in your next difficult conversation? What, specifically?

Check yourself

1. Your partner says, "You're late again. You obviously don't care about my time." Which horseman is that? What would a defensive reply sound like, and an accountable one?

Criticism: it moves from the lateness to a verdict about caring. Defensive: "I wasn't that late, and you're late all the time." Accountable: "You're right, I'm late, and I should have texted. I'm sorry." You can still raise the "obviously don't care" part later, once things are calm.

2. Predict first: why is "I need a break, let's talk at eight" not stonewalling?

Because it keeps the door open. It tells your partner you are coming back and when, so the break reads as looking after the conversation rather than rejecting them. Stonewalling is withdrawal with no signal that you will return.

3. A formula predicted divorce correctly about 90% of the time on the couples it was built from. Why might it do much worse on new couples?

Overfitting. A formula built on one group learns that group's quirks as well as any real pattern. Heyman and Slep's demographic formula fell from about 90% correct to being right only about 29% of the time when it predicted divorce for a fresh group.

4. Does better communication make couples happier, or do happier couples communicate better?

Some of both, but less than people assume. Lavner, Karney and Bradbury found that the links in each direction over time were weak, and that happiness predicting communication was the more common of the two.
