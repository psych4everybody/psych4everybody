Feeling Heard, Up Close

Why feeling understood matters so much, what to say when someone shares good news or bad, and how strong the evidence is.

About 12 minutes to readA one-week practiceBuilds on Section A

Which tells you more about whether a couple will stay together: how partners respond when one of them shares bad news, or when one shares good news?

Good news, surprisingly. In a study of dating couples, how partners responded to each other's good news was more closely linked to the health of the relationship, and to whether couples were still together two months later, than how they responded to bad news. Most people would guess the opposite.

Key points

  1. Feeling understood is one of the strongest ingredients of a good relationship. Researchers call it perceived partner responsiveness: the sense that your partner gets you, accepts you, and cares.
  2. It is the feeling that counts, not the technique. A response only builds closeness if the other person experiences it as understanding and caring.
  3. Good news is an overlooked opportunity. Celebrating with someone, actively and genuinely, is linked to closeness and trust.
  4. Support when things go wrong is harder than it looks. Advice, reassurance and fixing can all miss; asking what someone needs often works better.
  5. Formal "active listening" is useful, but it is not magic. Researchers still disagree about how much it helps once an argument is heated.

By the end of this page you will be able to

  • Explain what responsiveness is, and why perception matters more than intention.
  • Respond to good news in the way most linked to closeness.
  • Validate someone's feelings without agreeing with everything they say.
  • Offer support that fits what the other person actually wants.
  • Explain what the research on listening shows, and where it is still debated.
Part 1

Four moments that build, or break, closeness

And what you could say instead.

Section A introduced Reis and Shaver's intimacy process model: one person shares something that matters, the other responds, and closeness grows if the first person feels understood, validated and cared for. Here are four everyday moments where that loop either works or breaks.

Strong for the idea: perceived responsiveness is one of the most consistent predictors of relationship satisfaction.
Mixed for the specific scripts: they follow from the research, but most have not been tested one by one as things people can be taught.

1. When they share good news

What it is: Shelly Gable and colleagues call sharing good news capitalization. People share their best news of the day with someone most of the time, and how that person responds shapes what happens next.

Instead of"Nice. Did you remember to pick up the milk?"
Try responding actively and constructively"That's brilliant! When did you hear? What did they say? You must be so relieved."

Why this works: it shows you are glad, you are interested, and you want to relive it with them. In laboratory experiments by Harry Reis and colleagues, people whose good news got an enthusiastic response valued the event more and trusted the listener more, even when the listener was a stranger. "Nice" (passive) and "Won't that mean longer hours?" (active but deflating) both miss.

2. When they are upset

What it is: validation means letting someone know their feeling makes sense from where they stand. It does not require you to agree with them.

Instead of"You shouldn't let it get to you. It's only one meeting."
Try validating first"That sounds really frustrating, especially after all the work you put into it."

Why this works: "You shouldn't feel that" tells someone their reaction is wrong, so they either defend it or stop sharing. Validation lowers the temperature and tells them it is safe to keep talking. Rowland Miller's textbook describes communicating respect for a partner's point of view, whether or not you agree, as the key ingredient of good communication.

3. When you think you know what they mean

What it is: mindreading is assuming you understand without checking. Paraphrasing and perception checking are the alternatives: say back what you heard, and ask if you have it right.

Instead of"I know exactly what you mean. You just don't like my sister."
Try checking"So it's less about the visit, and more that it got decided without you? Have I got that right?"

Why this works: even partners who know each other well regularly misread each other. Checking does two jobs: it catches misunderstandings early, and it shows you care enough to get it right. If you have got it wrong, you find out before you argue about something they never said.

4. When something has gone wrong

What it is: social support comes in different kinds: emotional comfort, practical help, advice, and physical comfort such as a hug. Support helps most when it fits what the person needs right now.

Instead of"Right, here's what you should do. First, email your manager..."
Try asking what would help"That sounds awful. Do you want ideas, or do you mostly want to get it off your chest?"

Why this works: well-meant advice can feel like "you can't handle this." Gable's team found that responding well to bad news is harder than responding to good news; there is more that can go wrong. Miller's textbook sums up the research: the best support shows that you understand and care about what the person actually needs.

Sometimes the best support goes unnoticedIn a study of couples where one partner was preparing for a major professional exam, Niall Bolger and colleagues found that support the stressed partner didn't notice (practical help done quietly, for example) was linked to less distress than support they did notice. Very visible support can sometimes make people feel less capable.

Why the feeling matters more than the technique

Responsiveness lives in the eye of the receiver. That cuts both ways. A partner can be genuinely supportive and still not be felt as supportive: Miller's textbook reports that people with insecure attachment tend to rate their partners' help as less caring and less useful, even when it is genuinely good. And a clumsy response from someone you trust can land well.

So if your partner says "you're not listening", it is worth taking seriously even when you think you were. The question is not "did I use the right words?" but "did they come away feeling understood?"

Good times prepare you for bad timesGable's team has suggested that responding well to good news helps partners trust that you will be there when things go wrong. Good news is lower-stakes practice for being responsive.
Part 2

Try it

A small practice for one week.
Practice

A week of good-news responses Mixed

A few minutes a day, for 7 daysWhenever someone shares good newsEasy to start, harder to keep up

For one week, give every piece of good news an active, constructive response. Partners count, but so do friends, family, children and colleagues.

How to do it

  1. Listen for it. Good news is often small and slipped in passing: "The doctor said it's healing well", "I finally fixed the gate."
  2. Stop what you are doing. Put the phone down and turn towards them. Attention is half the response.
  3. Ask at least one question. "How did it happen?" "What did they say?" "How did you feel when you heard?" Questions let them relive it.
  4. Name what it means to them. "You've been worried about that for months; that must be a relief."
  5. Hold back the "but". Concerns about the new job's hours can wait for another day.
  6. At the end of the week, notice what changed. Did conversations last longer? Did people share more?

Why it works

Active, constructive responses tell people you are glad for them and interested in their lives. They are linked to more intimacy and satisfaction in couples, and in experiments they increase trust, even between strangers.

How good is the evidence?

Mixed. The link between good-news responses and closeness is well supported, including in studies where researchers watched couples rather than just asking them. The experiments with strangers suggest the response itself makes a difference. What has not been widely tested is whether practising it on purpose, as an exercise, improves a relationship over time.

Keep in mind. Genuine beats perfect. A forced "That's AMAZING!" can feel worse than a quiet, sincere "I'm really pleased for you." If someone's good news hurts you (a friend's pregnancy during your own fertility struggles, say), it is fine to be honest about mixed feelings later; you don't have to perform.
Part 3

What the research actually shows

Where these ideas come from, and where researchers disagree.
Inside the study

Gable, Gonzaga and Strachman (2006): "Will you be there for me when things go right?"

What they did

79 dating couples in the United States came to the lab. Each partner took turns telling the other about a recent positive event and a recent negative event, on video. The person sharing rated how understood, validated and cared for they felt, and trained observers coded how the partner responded. Two months later, the researchers checked how the couples were doing.

What they found

How partners responded to good news was linked to how well the relationships were doing, and to whether couples had broken up two months later. Responses to good news were more strongly linked to these outcomes than responses to bad news.

What it can't tell us
  • Who it applies to. These were dating couples in the US. Long-married couples and couples in other cultures may differ.
  • Cause and effect. Happier couples may simply respond more warmly to each other's news. The study shows the two go together, not that one causes the other.
  • The long run. Two months is a short follow-up. It cannot tell us what happens over years.

The active listening debate Mixed

For decades, couples programs taught active listening: paraphrase, reflect feelings, then reply. In 1998, John Gottman and colleagues reported a surprise from their study of 130 newlywed couples. Active listening hardly ever happened when couples argued, and it did not predict which couples did well. Gottman suggested it asks too much of people in the middle of a conflict, and called on therapists to stop relying on it.

Scott Stanley, Thomas Bradbury and Howard Markman disagreed. They called the recommendation premature at best, pointing to methodological and conceptual problems with the study. One question their critique raises: the fact that untrained couples rarely use a skill does not, by itself, show that the skill is useless once people are taught it.

Where does that leave you? Paraphrasing and validation clearly help people feel understood, which is the heart of responsiveness. They are easiest when emotions are not running high. In a heated moment, a break (see the four horsemen deep dive) may do more good than a perfectly formed paraphrase.

Does good communication cause a good relationship? Mixed

A study by Justin Lavner, Benjamin Karney and Thomas Bradbury followed more than 400 newlywed couples. At any one time, happier couples communicated better, but over time neither reliably predicted the other, and happiness predicting communication was the more common pattern. Responsiveness is a bit different from communication skill: it is about feeling understood, not technique. But the same caution applies. Being kind and attentive matters, and it is one part of a bigger picture.

Therapy-speak decoder

Words from this research have spread widely online, and their meanings have shifted on the way.

"I just need you to validate my feelings."
Online, it often means"Agree with me", or "tell me I'm right".
In the research, it meansRecognizing that a feeling makes sense from where the other person stands. You can validate someone's anger about a decision and still think the decision was right.
"Active listening means repeating back what they said."
Online, it often meansA script: "What I hear you saying is..."
In the research, it meansThe script is a tool, not the goal. What matters is whether the other person ends up feeling understood. A word-for-word echo can feel robotic.
"Holding space."
Online, it often meansBeing with someone in their pain without judging or fixing.
In the research, it meansThis is not a research term, but it overlaps with emotional support and validation. The research adds one thing: ask what kind of support they want, because sometimes people do want advice.

When this isn't the right tool

Everything on this page assumes that sharing is safe: that what you tell your partner will be met with care, even if clumsily. If sharing good news reliably brings mockery or punishment, if what you confide is later used against you, or if you are expected to validate everything your partner feels while your own feelings are dismissed, the problem is not listening skills.

Those can be signs of control rather than a communication gap. Read "When it is not a communication problem" in Section D. A domestic abuse service in your country can help you think through options safely. If you are in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number.

Check yourself

1. Your friend says, "I got the flat!" You reply, "Great. Isn't the rent a stretch, though?" Which kind of response is that, and what would an active-constructive one sound like?

Active-destructive: engaged, but focused on the downside. An active-constructive reply: "You got it! That's the one with the balcony, isn't it? When do you move in?"

2. Predict first: can you validate someone's feelings while disagreeing with them?

Yes. Validation says "your feeling makes sense from where you stand", not "you are right". For example: "I can see why you're annoyed I booked it without asking. I still think it was a good deal."

3. Why might a genuinely supportive partner still not feel supportive?

Because responsiveness is perceived. The support might not match what was needed (advice when comfort was wanted), or the person receiving it might, for example because of insecure attachment, tend to read support as less caring than it is.

4. Gottman found active listening rarely happened in real arguments. Does that prove it is useless?

No. Stanley, Bradbury and Markman called that conclusion premature, and untrained couples rarely using a skill does not, by itself, show the skill is useless once taught. It may be most useful when emotions are not running high.
