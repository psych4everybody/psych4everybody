The Demand-Withdraw Trap

Why one partner pushes and the other pulls away, why it isn't really about gender, and how couples get out of the loop.

About 12 minutes to readA 30-minute practice for twoBuilds on Section C

The "nagging wife, retreating husband" pattern is a cliché. When researchers watched gay and lesbian couples argue, did the same push-and-pull appear?

Yes, just as often. Sarah Holley and colleagues found the pattern in gay, lesbian and heterosexual couples alike. The best predictor of who pushed and who pulled away was not gender, but which partner wanted more change.

Key points

  1. Demand-withdraw is a loop, not a person. One partner presses for change; the other avoids. Each response makes the other's stronger.
  2. It is one of the most common conflict patterns, and one of the most damaging. A review of 74 studies linked it to lower satisfaction and intimacy, whichever partner does the demanding.
  3. Whoever wants the change tends to demand. Women demand more often in mixed-gender couples largely because they more often want something to change.
  4. Both roles make sense from the inside. The demander is trying not to be ignored; the withdrawer is trying to stop things getting worse.
  5. Context matters. For couples under heavy financial strain, withdrawing from demands that cannot be met may sometimes protect the relationship.

By the end of this page you will be able to

  • Recognize the demand-withdraw loop, including your own part in it.
  • Say what a demander and a withdrawer could each do differently.
  • Explain why the pattern follows who wants change more than gender.
  • Describe the evidence from same-sex couples and from different cultures.
  • Tell the ordinary loop apart from control.
Part 1

Both sides of the loop

What each partner can do to slow it down.

Section C introduced the demand-withdraw pattern: one partner raises a problem, criticizes or presses for change; the other changes the subject, goes quiet or leaves. The more one pushes, the more the other retreats, and the more they retreat, the harder the first one pushes. Here is what each side can do.

Strong for the pattern: it has been found across many studies, cultures and kinds of couples, and is reliably linked to distress.
Mixed for the alternatives: they come from couple therapies that target this loop, which have some support, but each step has not been tested on its own.

1. If you are the one who pushes

What it is: demanding: complaining, criticizing, and pressing for change, often louder as the other person retreats.

Instead of"We need to talk about this now. You always do this. You never want to deal with anything."
Try a specific request with a time"Your mum's visit is really on my mind. Can we take twenty minutes tonight to plan it?"

Why this works: "always" and "never" turn one issue into a verdict on the person, which gives them more reason to retreat. A specific topic, a limited time and a clear request make engaging feel manageable. It also helps to know that, to a withdrawer, pressure often feels like an attack, even when you are only trying to be heard.

2. If you are the one who pulls away

What it is: withdrawing: avoiding the topic, giving short answers, going silent or leaving.

Instead of"Whatever. Do what you want." (and leaving the room)
Try a "not now, but yes" with a time"I can't think straight about this right now, but I don't want to dodge it. Tomorrow after dinner?"

Why this works: to a demander, silence sounds like "you don't matter", which is exactly what makes them push harder. A delay with a promise and a time answers that fear. Then the most important part: keep the appointment, and if you can, raise the topic yourself. Starting the conversation is the clearest possible signal that you are not avoiding it.

3. Stepping out of the loop, together

What it is: noticing the pattern while it is happening, and naming it as something you are both caught in.

Instead of"You're doing it again. You never listen."
Try naming the loop, not the person"I think we're in our chase again: I'm pushing and you're backing off. Can we stop and try this a different way?"

Why this works: each partner usually sees the other as the cause. Naming the pattern makes it a shared problem rather than a fault in one person. This idea, sometimes called unified detachment, is central to integrative behavioral couple therapy, developed by Andrew Christensen and Neil Jacobson for exactly this kind of stuck loop.

4. When only one of you wants the change

What it is: the social structure side of the pattern: the partner who is happy with how things are has less reason to discuss changing them.

Instead of"You never want to change anything. You're so selfish."
Try acknowledging the imbalance"I know this matters more to me than to you right now. What would make it easier to think about?"

Why this works: in Christensen's research, both partners tended to demand when discussing a change they wanted, and to withdraw when discussing a change their partner wanted. Saying the imbalance out loud lowers the stakes for the other person and turns a standoff into a question you can work on.

Part 2

Try it

A conversation for two, on a calm day.
Practice

Name the chase Mixed

About 30 minutesFor two, on a calm dayModerate: needs both of you

A structured talk about the loop itself, not about any particular issue. Do it when nothing is currently being fought over.

How to do it

  1. Give the loop a name. Something neutral or even a little funny: "the chase", "the tug of war", "the dance". It is now a thing that happens to you both.
  2. Describe how it usually goes, step by step. What starts it? What does each of you do next? Try to describe it like an outsider watching.
  3. Each say what it feels like from inside. The pusher might say "When you go quiet, I feel like I don't count." The one who pulls away might say "When the pressure builds, I feel like nothing I say will be right." Listen without correcting.
  4. Each choose one change for your side. For the pusher: one topic at a time, with a time limit. For the one who pulls away: "not now" always comes with a time, and you sometimes start the conversation.
  5. Agree on a signal. A word either of you can use when you notice the chase starting: "We're doing the thing."
  6. Check in after a couple of weeks. Did the signal get used? What happened?

Why it works

The loop survives because each partner sees the other as the problem. Describing it together, and hearing what the other person's role feels like from inside, makes it easier to feel compassion instead of blame, and gives you a shared way to interrupt it.

How good is the evidence?

Mixed. The steps borrow from integrative behavioral couple therapy, which uses naming the pattern and understanding each other's experience of it. In a large trial with 134 seriously distressed married couples, that therapy and a more traditional skills-based therapy both helped many couples. The integrative approach did somewhat better over the two years after therapy ended, though the difference had faded by five years. Doing a version of this at home, without a therapist, has not been tested.

Keep in mind. This works best when both people want to try. If one of you feels too angry or hurt to describe the pattern fairly, it may help to have a couple therapist guide the first conversation. And it is a tool for an ordinary stuck loop, not for a relationship where one partner is afraid of the other (see the box below).
Part 3

What the research actually shows

Gender, power, culture, and money.
Inside the study

Holley, Sturm and Levenson (2010): is it about gender, or about wanting change?

What they did

Sarah Holley, Virginia Sturm and Robert Levenson studied 63 couples from the San Francisco Bay Area: 21 gay, 21 lesbian and 21 heterosexual, together for six to eight years on average. Each couple discussed an ongoing disagreement for 15 minutes on video. Trained coders rated each partner's demanding and withdrawing, and separate coders rated how much change each partner wanted.

What they found

All three kinds of couples showed the demand-withdraw pattern, to a similar degree. The strongest predictor of who demanded and who withdrew was the difference in how much change each partner wanted: the one who wanted more change tended to demand, and the one who wanted less tended to withdraw. Knowing whether a couple was gay, lesbian or heterosexual added nothing to the prediction.

What it can't tell us
  • Who it applies to. The couples were mostly young, white, monogamous and from one part of California. The same-sex and heterosexual couples were also recruited about 15 years apart.
  • Proof of "no difference". Failing to find a difference between groups is not the same as proving there is none. Small differences could exist that a study this size cannot detect.
  • The whole story of power. The study measured only one part of power, who wants change, and could not say why each partner demands or withdraws.

Who demands depends on whose issue it is Strong

Andrew Christensen and Christopher Heavey designed an elegant test. Each married couple had two discussions: one about a change the wife wanted, and one about a change the husband wanted. Across their studies, both partners tended to demand on their own issue and withdraw on their partner's. When the wife's issue was discussed, the classic wife-demand, husband-withdraw pattern was strong. When the husband's issue was discussed, the roles were much more even.

So why do women demand more often overall? Partly because, in mixed-gender couples, women more often want something to change, for example around housework and childcare. Andrew Christensen and colleagues found the same gender pattern in Brazil, Italy, Taiwan and the United States, which is consistent with an explanation based on the roles men and women commonly occupy, rather than something unique to one culture.

How much does it matter? Strong

Paul Schrodt and colleagues pooled 74 studies with over 14,000 participants. Demand-withdraw was moderately linked to worse outcomes: lower satisfaction, less intimacy, poorer communication, and more anxiety and aggression. The link was about the same strength whether the wife or the husband did the demanding, and it was stronger in distressed couples.

When withdrawing might help Mixed

Most research treats withdrawal as harmful. But Jaclyn Ross, Benjamin Karney, Teresa Nguyen and Thomas Bradbury looked at couples with very different resources, in two samples of over 400 couples each. For couples with more money and education, a wife's demands met with her husband's withdrawal predicted her satisfaction falling. For couples under heavy financial and social strain, the pattern reversed: withdrawal was linked to more stable satisfaction.

One possible reason: when a change genuinely cannot be made, for instance because there is no money for it, stepping back from the argument may protect the relationship better than fighting over it. The broader lesson is that a communication habit that hurts one couple can help another. Advice about "good communication" needs to fit people's real circumstances.

Therapy-speak decoder

This pattern has many names online. Here is what the research says about three of them.

"It's the anxious-avoidant trap."
Online, it often meansAn anxiously attached person and an avoidant person are doomed to chase and flee.
In the research, it meansAttachment insecurity can shape how people handle conflict, but demand-withdraw also depends heavily on the topic and on who wants change. The same person can demand on one issue and withdraw on another. It is a pattern, not a destiny.
"She's such a nag."
Online, it often meansA character flaw, usually blamed on women.
In the research, it meansThe demanding role goes to whoever wants change, in same-sex couples too. Calling it nagging puts the whole loop on one person, when the other partner's withdrawal is half of it.
"I just need space."
Online, it often meansA boundary that should never be questioned, or a polite way to end a conversation for good.
In the research, it meansSpace with a return time is a healthy way to calm down. Space with no return, used again and again whenever a topic comes up, is withdrawal, and it keeps the loop going.

When this isn't the right tool

The ordinary demand-withdraw loop is two people who both, in their own way, want the relationship to work. It is different when demands come with threats, intimidation or monitoring, or when silence is used for days on end as punishment, to make a partner anxious and compliant. Those are signs of control, not a stuck communication pattern.

Read "When it is not a communication problem" in Section D. A domestic abuse service in your country can help you think through options safely. If you are in immediate danger, contact your local emergency number.

  • Think of an issue that keeps coming back. Which of you wants the change more?
  • Is there one thing on this page you will actually do differently next time the loop starts? What, specifically?

Check yourself

1. Predict first: a couple discusses the husband's wish to move to another city. Who is more likely to demand?

The husband. Both men and women tend to demand on issues where they want the change, and withdraw on issues where their partner wants it.

2. Why does the loop keep itself going?

Each partner's response provokes more of the other's. Pushing makes withdrawing feel safer; withdrawing makes pushing feel necessary. And each partner tends to see the other as the cause.

3. What did Holley and colleagues find in gay and lesbian couples, and why does it matter?

The pattern appeared just as much as in heterosexual couples, and was best predicted by who wanted more change. That suggests the familiar gender pattern is mostly about wanting change and power, not about something built into men and women.

4. Why might withdrawal help some couples?

Ross and colleagues found that for couples under heavy financial strain, withdrawal from a partner's demands was linked to more stable satisfaction, perhaps because when a change cannot be made, stepping back protects the relationship better than fighting about it.
