Problems You Manage, Not Solve

Why some disagreements never go away, how couples live with them well, and when acceptance is the wrong answer.

About 12 minutes to readA 40-minute practice for twoBuilds on Section D

Married couples were asked about their recent arguments. What share came from built-in tensions, like wanting closeness and wanting independence, rather than from any one specific problem?

More than a third. In Leslie Erbert's study, four built-in tensions of close relationships accounted for over a third of married couples' recent arguments. Tensions like these never get settled once and for all; the balance just keeps shifting.

Key points

  1. Some disagreements come from differences that will not go away. How tidy a home should be, how much time to spend with family, how to handle money. Couples who do well tend to manage these, not solve them.
  2. Positions hide reasons. Behind "I want Christmas with my family" is something that matters, and the reasons are often easier to satisfy than the positions.
  3. Some tensions are built into closeness itself. Wanting connection and independence, openness and privacy, are not problems to fix but balances to keep adjusting.
  4. Acceptance is a real part of what helps. Couple therapy that adds emotional acceptance to working on change was tested in the largest and longest couple therapy trial to date, with encouraging results.
  5. Acceptance has limits. Differences in taste or temperament are for accepting. Contempt, control and fear are not.

By the end of this page you will be able to

  • Tell a solvable problem from a lasting difference.
  • Find the reasons underneath a position, yours and your partner's.
  • Name the four built-in tensions of close relationships and ways couples manage them.
  • Explain what the best long-term couple therapy trial found about acceptance.
  • Say when acceptance is the wrong response.
Part 1

Four ways to live with a difference

And what you could say instead.

Section D introduced perpetual problems, relational dialectics and integrative agreements. Here is how each shows up in an ordinary argument, with something you could try instead.

Strong for the idea that some tensions are lasting: research on dialectics and on long-term couples consistently finds recurring disagreements, even in happy relationships.
Mixed for the specific techniques: they come from negotiation research and couple therapy, and have not each been tested on their own.

1. When the same argument comes back

What it is: a perpetual problem: a disagreement rooted in a lasting difference in personality, values or habits. John Gottman argues that many couple disagreements are of this kind.

Instead of"We've been over this a hundred times. Why can't you just change?"
Try managing it for now"Here we are again with the tidiness thing. Can we work out how to live with it this month, rather than settle it forever?"

Why this works: expecting to "win" a perpetual problem guarantees a fight every time it surfaces. Treating it as something you will both keep adjusting takes away the pressure for one person to change who they are. Gottman's observation is that couples who do well keep talking about these differences with humor and affection, and without contempt.

2. When you are stuck on positions

What it is: arguing over positions (what each person says they want) instead of interests (why they want it). The distinction comes from negotiation research, made famous by Roger Fisher and William Ury.

Instead of"We're spending Christmas with my family. End of story."
Try sharing the reason"For me, Christmas with my family is about Mum. She's getting older and I don't know how many more there'll be. What does Christmas at home mean to you?"

Why this works: two positions can be impossible to reconcile while the reasons behind them are not. Maybe one partner needs time with an ageing parent and the other needs a rest from travel; a visit just before Christmas and a quiet day at home could meet both. Rowland Miller's textbook describes the recipe for integrative agreements as stubbornness about goals, combined with flexibility about how to reach them.

3. When you want two opposite things

What it is: a dialectic: a tension between two needs that both matter, such as connection and independence.

Instead of"You'd obviously rather be with your friends than with me."
Try giving each side its own space"I've been missing time with just us. Could we keep Friday nights for us, and Saturdays for friends?"

Why this works: the first version turns a normal tension into a test of love. The second accepts that both needs are legitimate and gives each one a place, a strategy Leslie Baxter called segmentation. Others include cyclic alternation (taking turns over time) and reframing (seeing how the two sides support each other: "Having my own friends makes me a better partner").

4. When the difference is part of who they are

What it is: emotional acceptance: coming to see a partner's difficult trait as understandable, and sometimes even as the flip side of something you value.

Instead of"If you really cared, you'd stop being late all the time."
Try acceptance plus a workaround"You're never going to be an early person, and I'm never going to relax about time. Shall we just tell you we're leaving fifteen minutes before we are?"

Why this works: repeated demands to change a basic trait usually produce resentment, not change. Accepting the difference, and designing around it, takes the trait out of the fight. Brian Doss and Andrew Christensen found that the more accepting partners are of each other's frustrating behavior, the more satisfied they tend to be.

Acceptance is not resignationIn integrative behavioral couple therapy, acceptance sits alongside change, not instead of it. The aim is to change what can be changed, and to stop fighting over what cannot.
Part 2

Try it

A structured conversation about one recurring disagreement.
Practice

What's underneath Mixed

About 40 minutesFor two, on a calm dayModerate: needs real listening

Pick one disagreement that keeps coming back, not the most painful one. The goal is not to solve it today, but to understand what it means to each of you.

How to do it

  1. Agree on the topic and the rules. One topic only. For each turn, one person talks and the other only asks questions: no persuading, no rebuttals.
  2. First speaker, about ten minutes: explain what your position means to you. Is there a story behind it, a value, a fear, a hope? Where does it come from in your life?
  3. Listener: ask curious questions. "What would it mean to you if we did it your way?" "Is there a part of this that matters most?" At the end, sum up what you heard.
  4. Swap. Same rules, other way round.
  5. Look for options that meet both sets of reasons. Brainstorm without judging first, then pick something to try.
  6. If there is no option yet, agree how to live with it for now. And agree when to revisit it. Managing well is a good result.

Why it works

Arguments about positions are win-or-lose. Talking about reasons turns the other person from an opponent into someone with understandable needs, and often reveals options neither of you had considered. Even when nothing is solved, understanding why something matters so much to your partner makes the difference easier to accept.

How good is the evidence?

Mixed. The interests-behind-positions idea is well established in negotiation research, much of it outside couples. The idea of looking for the deeper meaning in a stuck disagreement is part of Gottman's clinical approach, and understanding each other's experience is central to integrative behavioral couple therapy. This exact conversation, done at home, has not been tested as a stand-alone exercise.

Keep in mind. Start with a medium-sized disagreement, not the one that hurts most. If you hit something very painful, such as a betrayal, a loss, or a decision about children, it may be worth having a couple therapist help. And some differences are dealbreakers for good reasons; understanding each other better does not oblige either of you to give up something essential.
Part 3

What the research actually shows

The longest couple therapy trial, and how solid the famous numbers are.
Inside the study

Christensen and colleagues (2010): change, acceptance, and five years later

What they did

Andrew Christensen and colleagues randomly assigned 134 seriously and chronically distressed married couples, in Los Angeles and Seattle, to one of two therapies. Traditional behavioral couple therapy focused on change: communication and problem-solving skills. Integrative behavioral couple therapy used similar techniques but added work on emotional acceptance and understanding each other's sensitivities. Couples had up to 26 sessions over a year and were then followed up every six months for five years.

What they found

Right after therapy, about two-thirds of couples had improved significantly. The acceptance-based therapy did better over the first two years of follow-up, though the advantage was modest and faded later. Five years on, about half the couples were significantly better than when they started, about a quarter had separated or divorced, and about a quarter were unchanged.

What it can't tell us
  • What would have happened without therapy. Both groups had therapy; there was no untreated comparison group, so we cannot say how many couples would have improved anyway.
  • Who it applies to. These were married, seriously distressed couples in two US cities. Less distressed couples, unmarried couples and other cultures may respond differently.
  • Whether acceptance works without a therapist. The couples had trained therapists. Trying to accept a partner's traits on your own is a different thing.

How solid is "69% of problems are perpetual"? Mixed

You may have seen the claim that about 69% of couple conflicts are perpetual. It comes from Gottman's observations in his clinical and research work and is best treated as a rough estimate, not a measured fact. What is better supported is the broader idea: many disagreements recur for years, even in happy couples, and research on dialectics finds built-in tensions behind a large share of arguments.

Acceptance and change both matter Mixed

Researchers analysing the same trial looked at how couples improved. Changes in communication, changes in how often frustrating behaviors happened, and growing emotional acceptance of those behaviors all played a part. The traditional therapy produced bigger changes in the behaviors themselves, while acceptance was a distinctive focus of the integrative approach.

The practical lesson is that "they should just change" and "I should just accept it" are both incomplete. Most couples who improve do some of each.

Therapy-speak decoder

Three ideas about compatibility that come up whenever an old argument does.

"We're just incompatible."
Online, it often meansIf we keep disagreeing, we're wrong for each other.
In the research, it meansEvery couple has lasting differences. Miller's textbook describes "destiny" beliefs (partners are either meant for each other or not) as unhelpful, and "growth" beliefs (relationships take work) as more realistic and better for couples.
"Compromise means you both lose."
Online, it often meansNever meet halfway; hold out for what you want.
In the research, it meansCompromise, where both give something up, is one way conflicts end. Integrative agreements, where both people's underlying goals are met, are often better, and are easier to find when you talk about reasons rather than positions.
"Acceptance means settling."
Online, it often meansGiving up on what you deserve.
In the research, it meansIn couple therapy, acceptance means no longer fighting over traits that are part of who someone is, while still working on what can change. It does not mean tolerating mistreatment.

When this isn't the right tool

Acceptance is for differences: tidiness, punctuality, sociability, how to spend a weekend. It is not for contempt, threats, controlling money or movements, monitoring your phone, or anything that makes you afraid. Those are not perpetual problems to live with, and no amount of understanding "what's underneath" makes them acceptable.

Think about it

  • Think of a disagreement you have had for years. Is it something to solve, or a difference to manage?
  • Which of the four built-in tensions (closeness and independence, openness and privacy, routine and novelty, time together and time with others) pulls hardest in your closest relationship?
  • Is there one thing on this page you will actually do differently? What, specifically?

Check yourself

1. Predict first: what is the difference between a position and an interest?

A position is what someone says they want ("We're going to my parents'"). An interest is why they want it ("I'm worried about my dad's health"). Interests are often easier to satisfy together than positions.

2. A couple agrees that Tuesdays are for the gym with friends and Fridays are for each other. Which way of managing a dialectic is that?

Segmentation: giving each side of the tension (independence and connection) its own time or area of life.

3. What did the five-year couple therapy trial find, and what can't it tell us?

About half the seriously distressed couples were significantly better five years on, and the acceptance-based therapy had a modest early advantage. Because both groups got therapy, it cannot tell us how many would have improved without it.

4. Give an example of a difference to accept, and one that should not be accepted.

To accept: one partner is a homebody and the other loves going out. Not to accept: a partner who checks your phone, controls the money, or makes you afraid. That is control, not a difference in temperament.
